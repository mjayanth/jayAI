01 · Platform thesis

Build the capabilities teams keep needing.

Share identity, data access, evaluation and operating controls. Keep business definitions, workflow decisions and accountability with the people who understand the work.

Start with the work.

Name the user, current workflow, decision, consequence of failure and definition of an accepted result before discussing architecture.

Share what teams can reuse.

Identity, retrieval, model access, evaluation and traces should be shared. The domain experience may remain purpose-built.

Test the complete workflow.

Models supply capacity. Contracts, evals, hard rules, human approvals and recovery paths determine what can safely ship.

Controls follow intended use.

A writing assistant, a financial decision aid and a device-adjacent workflow do not belong in one undifferentiated risk class.

Every run creates evidence.

Traces support debugging, audit, cost analysis and learning. Capture required records during the workflow, with their own retention and access controls. Operational traces alone are not the audit record.

Adoption is part of delivery.

A technically correct capability that people route around is unfinished. Workflow fit, trust and ownership belong on the roadmap.

Build as platform when…

  • several teams have validated the same need
  • inconsistency creates material security, quality or cost risk
  • shared evidence improves every downstream use case
  • the capability has a stable contract and a clear owning team

Keep local when…

  • domain judgment is the differentiator
  • the workflow is still being discovered
  • the latency, safety or experience constraint is unique
  • forced reuse would add more coordination than it saves

02 · Product portfolio

Decide which work is worth doing.

Group requests by the business outcome and the capabilities they need. Agree priorities with business, engineering, data and control owners. Look for common needs without forcing every team into the same workflow.

A

Knowledge work

Search, synthesis, Q&A and governed knowledge bases. Optimize accepted-answer rate, provenance and time recovered.

B

Decision support

Text-to-SQL, analysis and recommendations. Measure correctness, appropriate abstention and reviewer effort.

C

Workflow automation

Agents that use tools and change systems. Optimize successful task completion, safe recovery and cost per task.

D

Embedded intelligence

AI inside products or regulated workflows. Optimize validated performance, integration quality and lifecycle evidence.

Hypothetical scenario

Three teams want help this quarter. Engineering can support one new shared capability alongside existing commitments. The scenarios below assume the named owners and evidence exist. Verify both before committing.

Build a shared capability

Document intake

Two teams handle similar document types. They have reviewed examples, measurable rework and people available to test the output.

Share extraction, checks and evidence. Keep each team’s fields, exceptions and approval process separate. Start with one document type.

Reuse or buy

Translation in an existing tool

An approved supplier may already cover the workflow. Test its output with domain reviewers and check access, retention, integration and total cost.

Adopt it if it meets the same acceptance criteria. Build only the missing capability that the evidence justifies.

Defer automated action

Financial posting

The team wants autonomous posting, but accounting rules, approval authority and recovery procedures are unresolved.

Resolve those dependencies first. Consider a read-only pilot if it has value on its own; keep posting outside the pilot.

Make the trade-off visible

Product recommends the sequence. Engineering sizes delivery and support; data owners assess readiness; control owners set release conditions. The business sponsor decides between competing outcomes within the agreed capacity. A mandatory control cannot be traded away for a higher benefit score.

Record what was decided

Outcome and baseline · Evidence confidence · Capacity and dependencies · Chosen option · Work deferred · Decision owner · Review date · What would change the decision

Tell the team whose request waits

Explain the constraint, what was chosen and why. Offer a safe alternative when one exists, name the dependency owner and set a review date. Revisit the decision when evidence changes. A new executive request should show what it displaces before it enters the roadmap.

Expand to another region or improve the current release?

Separate a missing feature from a reason people cannot use the product. Compare both options on the same outcome, quality and support evidence.

Improve the current release

Investigate whether users leave because of wrong answers, unclear permissions, extra review work or a poor fit with their workflow. Fix a material failure before repeating it elsewhere.

Test the next region

Proceed with a small cohort when the current capability solves their task and local data, language, controls and support are ready. Reuse the shared capability; evaluate local examples separately.

Regional representatives help set the criteria and review results. Product publishes the choice, the work displaced and the next review point. Location alone does not determine priority.

Eight flows through the system

The numbers match the diagram.

  1. 1

    Ask

    A request arrives in the user’s workflow. Identity establishes who is asking and their access. The router passes the request and permitted context to the orchestrator.

  2. 2

    Plan and delegate

    The orchestrator assigns work to domain agents, which call shared capabilities. Each task carries its owner, acceptance criteria, allowed tools, budget and stopping conditions.

  3. 3

    Ground

    The tool gateway checks access on every call. Retrieval returns permitted evidence with source versions and citations. Missing or conflicting evidence triggers clarification or escalation.

  4. 4

    Reason

    The model gateway routes to an approved model and region, applies data-handling controls, and records usage. Evaluate the route against the task’s quality, latency and cost requirements.

  5. 5

    Act with approval

    Show the proposed action and evidence to an authorized person. Execute only the approved action through a scoped tool. The owning system validates the write; verify its result and prevent duplicate execution.

  6. 6

    Refresh the knowledge

    Data pipelines prepare governed datasets from source systems and update indexes, graphs and models. Preserve lineage and source permissions. User feedback does not automatically authorize retraining or a policy change.

  7. 7

    Evaluate

    Check inputs, retrieval, tool calls and outcomes. Use offline suites before release and risk-appropriate checks during operation. Failed checks block, retry within limits, or escalate with the evidence.

  8. 8

    Govern

    Enforce policy at the control points. Link approvals, versions, actions and results with a run ID. Keep required audit records on a durable path, with retention, access controls and tamper protection.

04 · Agent delivery workflow

How agents help build and review software.

The delivery workflow takes a request through a specification, implementation and review. Keep the evidence needed to inspect a run. Safe replay also needs retained inputs and versions, with real business actions disabled.

Operating model as versioned config

Task types, templates, golden sets, guidelines, thresholds, risk floors and model routes change without rewriting the pipeline.

Deterministic first, judgment second

Tests, static analysis, graph rules and policy checks run before a model judge. A judge can raise risk; it cannot lower a hard floor.

Cross-model verification

Where model evaluation helps, compare evaluators against domain reviewers. A different model family can reduce some shared blind spots; it does not guarantee an independent or correct verdict.

Spec failure is a first-class outcome

Repeated failure triggers a review of the specification, context, tools and model. Cap retries instead of continuing without new evidence.

Human intent and risk anchors

Requesters confirm acceptance criteria. The authorized owner signs off before changes are merged or released, with any additional process approvals.

Every decision is traced

Routes, artifact references, checks, costs and approvals share a run identifier. Minimize sensitive payloads and retain required audit records separately.

Proposed workflow

A business user asks, “Why did service revenue fall last quarter?” Start with an approved metric and a limited set of questions the team needs answered. Put the experience in an existing analyst workspace, collaboration tool or application when that is where the work happens.

  1. Clarify the questionConfirm the revenue definition, period, region and comparison. Keep these choices visible as the conversation continues.
  2. Use governed dataResolve approved metrics, joins and access in the existing data platform. Retrieve permitted definitions or policy documents only when they help explain the result.
  3. Run a bounded queryUse a read-only identity with the user’s effective access. Validate the query, restrict scope and set execution limits before running it. A follow-up question requires another access check.
  4. Show the basis for the answerReturn the result, filters, data freshness and query reference. Distinguish a numerical decomposition from a proven cause; correlated changes alone do not establish causation.
  5. Check and continueCompare known questions with approved results, test ambiguous follow-ups and denied-access cases, and ask for clarification when the evidence is insufficient.

A semantic layer holds agreed business definitions. Retrieval can supply explanations; a graph may help resolve relationships. Add either when it improves measured task performance enough to justify its operating cost. An MCP interface exposes approved tools; each tool still enforces its own authorization.

Evaluate the organization’s existing analytics service before building a new text-to-SQL engine. Use the same question set, permissions tests and cost model for both options.

06 · Agents & tools

Define what the agent can do and when it stops.

Use an agent when the next step depends on what it finds. Give it approved tools, a budget and a recovery path. Before it changes a business system or finalizes a deliverable, the user signs off on the exact work.

Confirm task and authority
Read permitted evidence
Propose the exact action
Approve, then execute
Verify the result
Finish, recover or escalate

Runtime contract

  • identity and delegated authority
  • allowed tools and argument schemas
  • time, token, cost and action budgets
  • idempotency key and rollback strategy
  • stop conditions and escalation owner

Agent lease

In this reference design, a lease is a time-bounded grant tying one agent, user, purpose, environment, tool set and spend ceiling together. Enforce it at tool boundaries alongside source-system permissions.

  • expires delegated access unless renewed
  • separates exploration from production
  • requires tested expiry, revocation and attribution

Use an agent when

  • the task requires several conditional steps
  • the environment returns useful observations
  • success can be verified after each action
  • failure has a bounded recovery or escalation path

Prefer a workflow when

  • the sequence is stable and known
  • determinism matters more than flexibility
  • a simple service or rule engine is sufficient
  • the action has no safe observation loop

Determine the assurance path before building

“Internal” and “human-reviewed” are not risk classifications. A draft can affect a regulated record; a recommendation can affect a material financial decision. Start with intended use and the consequence of failure.

  1. Document the use. Name the users, decisions, records and systems affected. State what the product must not do.
  2. Agree the review path. Quality, Security, Privacy and the relevant business control owner determine which requirements apply. Clinical or device functionality needs a separate assessment from an internal productivity tool.
  3. Trace requirements to evidence. Keep approved requirements, risk assessments, test results, unresolved limitations, reviewer authority and release decisions together. Determine record retention and signature requirements with the accountable owners.
  4. Control change. Assess changes to models, prompts, sources, tools and workflows. Re-test the affected behavior and obtain the required approvals before release. Keep a recovery path and investigate incidents.

FDA’s Computer Software Assurance guidance describes a risk-based approach for software used in medical-device production or quality management systems. It is not a blanket approval for enterprise AI. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary guidance for managing AI risk, not a certification.

Trace design rule

Record the evidence, versions, checks and outcome needed to investigate a decision. Exact replay needs additional retained state and may not reproduce a model’s output. Keep sensitive payloads out of general telemetry and use a durable path for required audit records.

See how to test a claim, an answer and an action
Hypothetical evaluation cases

A working citation proves that a source exists. Checking the claim requires the relevant passage, source version and context. Keep automated checks and human judgment separate in the evidence.

Check the claim

“Returns are allowed for 60 days.”

The approved policy says 30 days. Linking to that policy does not make the answer correct. Extract the claim, locate the supporting passage and compare the value, units, conditions and effective date.

A deterministic comparison can catch this mismatch. An ambiguous exception still needs clarification or qualified review.

Check the answer

“Revenue fell last quarter.”

A valid query may use the wrong metric, currency, population or period. Compare the result with an approved query on a controlled dataset and test follow-up questions that change one constraint.

Check access separately. Correct totals do not prove that the user was allowed to see the underlying data.

Check the action

“Update this customer record.”

Bind approval to the exact fields, values and record version. Test revoked access, stale approvals, invalid inputs, duplicate requests and tool failures.

Read back the authoritative result. A successful tool response alone does not establish that the intended business change occurred.

Measure the evaluator too

  • Build a versioned, expert-reviewed test set with routine cases, difficult exceptions, missing evidence and deliberately wrong outputs. Keep a held-out set separate from prompt tuning.
  • Report missed errors and false alarms by severity and task type. Show counts and denominators, judge–reviewer disagreement and how disagreements were resolved.
  • Agree acceptance thresholds before the release test. An aggregate score must not hide a failed access control, a critical error or a weak user cohort.
  • Measure reviewer effort and correction quality. Human approval is a control that needs clear evidence, time and authority; it is not a guarantee of correctness.
Release evidence

Intended use · Test-set version and coverage · Results by failure type · Failed cases and limitations · Model, prompt, source and tool versions · Reviewer decision · Rollback owner

Know what changed

To assess a new model, hold the retrieval, tools and test set steady. To assess a new workflow, keep the model fixed where possible. Record provider changes that cannot be controlled. Better model capability and better orchestration are different explanations for an improved result.

During operation, reject missing authorization, pause unsupported consequential claims and route ambiguous cases to a reviewer. Retry only when there is a reason another attempt could succeed, with a fixed limit. Zero observed failures in a finite test set does not establish zero risk.

09 · Adoption & operations

Adoption is a function of trust and value.

Start with a narrow task and the people doing it. Show the result, listen to what changes for them and measure the work after review. Useful output alone will not settle concerns about accountability, workload or job changes.

01

Observe

Watch the current work. Quantify handoffs, shadow systems, delays and review burden.

02

Co-design

Build with the people who perform and approve the work; encode their exceptions.

03

Prove

Run in shadow or limited release; show accepted results and honest failure modes.

04

Embed

Place the capability in the system of work, with support, ownership and a fallback.

05

Retire

Remove the old route only when reliability and trust are sufficient; track exceptions.

Federated operating model

  • platform team owns shared interfaces, supported delivery paths, controls and evidence
  • domain product owns workflow, acceptance criteria and adoption
  • data owner owns meaning, quality and approved use
  • risk and quality owners set minimum controls and independently review evidence

Decision rights

  • platform reviews duplication and proposes shared options
  • domain owners decide whether the workflow meets their needs
  • control owners approve changes to required safeguards
  • product council allocates shared capacity using evidence
Agree product and engineering ownership

Technical fluency helps product managers ask better questions, test assumptions and explain trade-offs. Teams still need explicit decision rights and a shared definition of a useful result.

Product owns

Problem framing, discovery, outcome measures, priority recommendations, acceptance criteria, stakeholder communication and adoption. Product stays accountable after launch.

Engineering owns

Technical design, implementation, estimates, reliability and supportability. Engineers challenge a proposed approach and identify safer or simpler alternatives.

Business and data owners own

Process decisions, metric definitions, source quality, reviewer capacity and whether the outcome works in practice. They supply exceptions as well as happy-path examples.

Control owners own

Applicable security, privacy, quality and regulatory requirements, required evidence and approval authority. Product and engineering build these into delivery.

Use a prototype to answer a question

Write down the hypothesis, the result and what the prototype did not test. Review it with engineering before making a delivery commitment. Its code and architecture are options; the agreed user need and acceptance criteria are the inputs to production design.

Resolve a disagreement

Compare options against user impact, risk, delivery time and operating cost. Time-box an experiment when evidence is missing. Name the decision owner, record the choice and state what would justify revisiting it. Escalate the unresolved trade-off with options, rather than asking an executive to settle an undefined argument.

When people are reluctant to use the product

Ask privately what worries people before assuming they need more training. A person may understand the benefit and still fear extra review work, loss of autonomy, surveillance or a change to their role.

  1. Understand the concernInclude regular users, skeptics, managers and reviewers. Observe where the current process protects them or gives them control. Avoid treating non-use as a performance problem.
  2. Agree the observation boundaryExplain what the assistant can read, what is logged, who sees those logs and how long they are kept. Offer a way to pause observation. Do not quietly repurpose usage data for employee evaluation.
  3. Start alongside the userRun in shadow mode against permitted tasks. Compare drafts with the existing process without writing to business systems or forcing users onto the new route.
  4. Make approval meaningfulShow the proposed change, evidence and uncertainty. Keep accept, edit, reject and escalate available. Recheck permissions and require sign-off on the exact action before execution.
  5. Agree what changes in the jobManagers and people teams own workforce decisions. Product makes workload and role impacts visible, supports training and avoids promises about job security it cannot make.
  6. Expand from observed resultsTrack sustained use, corrections, review minutes and reasons people return to the old process. Increase scope only when users and accountable owners accept the results and support is ready.

Personalization can change presentation, task defaults and approved context. It must not silently broaden access, change policy or authorize actions.

Run the platform after the pilot

Use existing decision forums where they work. The cadence below is a starting point, not a reason to create another committee.

  • Intake and discovery: Record the workflow, affected users, baseline, owner, risk and alternatives. Group related requests before committing to another agent.
  • Weekly delivery review: Product, engineering, data and domain owners review outcomes, dependencies, capacity and unresolved decisions. Reserve capacity for reliability and mandatory controls.
  • Release review: Inspect task-level evals, approval evidence, support readiness and rollback. Name the person who can pause or withdraw the release.
  • Monthly portfolio review: Compare accepted outcomes, sustained use, support load and cost. Fund, adjust, consolidate or stop work using the same published criteria.
  • Incident response: Stop affected actions when necessary, preserve evidence, notify the right owners and restore a safe workflow. Verify the fix and add the failure to regression tests.

Give locally built agents a path into supported operation: register an owner and intended use, compare existing capabilities, review permissions, run evals, name support and plan retirement. Find duplicates before asking teams to rewrite working tools.

10 · Metrics & economics

Measure the result after review and rework.

A cheap model call can produce an expensive business outcome. A costly call can be efficient if it prevents rework. Normalize economics around a completed, accepted task.

Outcome scorecard

accepted-result rate = accepted tasks ÷ attempted tasks
  • time from intent to accepted result
  • rework and reviewer minutes per result
  • sustained weekly use in the target cohort
  • business effect versus baseline

Unit economics

cost / successful task = (model + tools + infra + evals + support + review + rework) ÷ accepted tasks
  • segment by task type and risk tier
  • include failed runs and escalations
  • compare model routes on total cost, not token price
  • budget and meter; efficiency alone can expand demand

Platform reuse

reuse rate = eligible products using a shared capability ÷ eligible products
  • time to onboard a new product
  • percentage using paved-road contracts
  • duplicate services retired
  • upgrade adoption and backward-compatibility burden

Trust & quality

material failure rate = released tasks with a material failure ÷ completed released tasks
  • abstention appropriateness
  • judge-human agreement
  • policy and authorization violations
  • rollback, recovery and incident time
Estimate capacity and model cost without overstating savings

Separate the team building it from the team using it

A delivery estimate covers engineering, product, data, security, quality and support work. An operating-capacity estimate covers the tasks people perform after launch. Faster prototyping does not by itself establish either production staffing or workforce savings.

Measure recovered time on comparable work

Net minutes per task = baseline task time − assisted task time − additional review and rework time.

Count each activity once. Include failed attempts and manual fallbacks across the evaluated workload. Preserve negative results. Multiply observed net time by comparable volume only after checking the task mix.

Distinguish an estimate, a pilot result and a sustained production outcome. Recovered hours become financial savings only when spending changes or the extra capacity produces measurable value. Report the period, cohort, denominator and attribution limits.

Choose a model route against the task

Test a deterministic rule or service first when the task allows it. Compare a classifier or ranker, a smaller model and a larger generative model on the same acceptance criteria. Preserve a fallback when a cheaper route cannot handle the task.

Self-hosted open-weight models may offer control over deployment and customization. Include serving, hardware utilization, patching, security, upgrades, evaluation and on-call work in the comparison. Open weights do not imply CPU-only operation; measure latency and throughput on the intended hardware and workload.

Treat research benchmarks as candidates for local testing. A reported speedup for ranking cached choices is not an end-to-end speedup for a business workflow. Keep experimental routes separate from production until they pass the required checks.

11 · Roadmap

An example first-six-month roadmap.

Improve the platform already in use before proposing a replacement. The timing below is illustrative; existing commitments, data readiness, risk review and delivery capacity determine the sequence.

0–30 days

Listen and baseline

  • meet users, owners, skeptics and support teams
  • map existing capabilities, commitments and decision rights
  • agree one workflow baseline and its acceptance criteria
  • prioritize gaps and escalate immediate risks to their owners
31–60 days

Test one improvement

  • reuse existing identity, gateways, data and delivery tools
  • close the gaps needed for this workflow
  • run a shadow pilot with task evals and review time measured
  • decide to release, revise or stop against agreed criteria
61–90 days

Prove reuse

  • onboard a second team with a comparable need
  • test permissions, local exceptions and support load
  • document shared contracts and ownership
  • publish outcomes and explain what remains deferred
90–180 days

Expand selectively

  • extend to ready cohorts and regions
  • compare model routes on quality and total cost
  • consolidate duplicate services with their owners
  • retire old routes only after migration and recovery are tested

Expansion is a decision gate, not a date on a slide. If quality, user benefit or support readiness falls short, narrow the scope, fix the gap or stop. Keep stakeholders informed about what changes and why.

13 · Failure patterns

Problems to watch for.

Review these as the work moves from a prototype into a supported service.

Gateway as platform

Model access without identity, context, evaluation, traces and product ownership leaves teams rebuilding the rest.

Demo-to-production jump

Documents and committees sit outside delivery, so teams route around them. Encode control floors and evidence in paved roads.

One interface for every workflow

A common surface is mistaken for common capability. Domain workflows become shallow while local tools proliferate underneath.

Agent without a lease

Long-lived credentials, unclear ownership and no budget or stop condition create unbounded operational risk.

Token cost without review cost

Cheaper calls look efficient while retries, review, rework and failed tasks make the business outcome more expensive.

Judge grades itself

Model evaluation substitutes for tests and calibrated human review. Changing model families alone does not resolve this.

Adoption by mandate

Training and executive sponsorship substitute for workflow fit, trust, support and the hard work of retiring the old route.
